What the project is trying to do

Sri Guru Granth Sahib contains roughly 60,000 lines of scripture. A reader who wants to find every verse about, say, letting go of envy has a problem: the same teaching appears in many places, in different words, across different musical measures and hundreds of pages apart.

Ordinary search cannot help, because ordinary search matches words. Type a verse about envy and you get back verses containing that verse's particular words — not verses carrying that verse's meaning.

Our system is meant to match meaning, which in Gurbani is called Bhavarth. Give it one Pankati (line), and it returns other Pankatis that teach the same thing, with their commentary, their Ang (page number), and their Raag.

Here is the whole problem in two lines

These two verses appear 700 pages apart. They have not a single word in common. They say the same thing.

ਵਿਸਰੀ ਤਿਸੈ ਪਰਾਈ ਤਾਤਾ ॥
"He forgets to feel envy of others."
ANG 189
ਛਾਡਿ ਵਿਡਾਣੀ ਤਾਤਿ ਮੂੜੇ ॥
"O fool, give up your envy of others."
ANG 889
In plain terms

A keyword search will never connect these two lines, because there is nothing to match. A system that understands meaning would connect them instantly. That gap is the entire project.

The five stages, and where we are

The work divides into five stages. All five are built and measured. The sixth — actually teaching the model Gurbani — has not started, and the reason is explained at the end.

1

Reading the data before touching it

Before writing a single line of cleaning code

The dataset has one row per verse, with up to five different scholars' commentaries — called Steeks — attached to each. The obvious move is to start cleaning. We measured first instead, and found three things that would each have quietly damaged the project.

Discovery one: two of the five commentaries are the same

Of the rows where both were present, 99.9% — 54,941 out of 55,017 — were character-for-character identical between the columns steek_ss and steek_bdb. It is one commentary stored twice, not two opinions.

Why it matters

If you teach from a duplicated textbook, that book's lessons count double. Every verse covered by that commentary would have quietly carried twice the influence of a verse covered by the others — a bias invisible in any result.

Discovery two: one "commentary" is a dictionary

49,928 of 50,015 entries in steek_pss contain an equals sign. They are word-by-word glossaries, not explanations:

ਰੈਣਿ = ਰਾਤ, ਜ਼ਿੰਦਗੀ-ਰੂਪ ਰਾਤ। ਪਹਿਲੈ ਪਹਰੈ = ਪਹਿਲੇ ਪਹਰ ਵਿਚ।
A vocabulary list — "night = night, the night that is life" — not a statement of the verse's teaching.
Why it matters

Training a meaning-matcher on vocabulary lists teaches it to recognise the shape of a dictionary entry. It would have actively damaged the ability we are trying to build. We kept it for display — it is genuinely useful to a reader — but excluded it from teaching.

So the dataset's advertised "five commentaries per verse" is really three independent ones: Sahib Singh (modern Punjabi), Faridkot (older Braj), and Manmohan Singh (concise Punjabi).

Discovery three: thousands of headings were being treated as scripture

The dataset marks 720 rows as structural. The true figure is around 5,000. Lines like ੴ ਸਤਿਗੁਰ ਪ੍ਰਸਾਦਿ ॥ (522 times), ਪਉੜੀ ॥ (515 times) and ਆਸਾ ॥ (39 times) are titles and section markers — the scripture's equivalent of chapter headings.

Why it matters

Repeated headings confuse a learning system badly. The same heading appears hundreds of times attached to hundreds of different passages, so the system receives constant contradictory instruction about what it means.

2

Cleaning, without deleting anything

Every exclusion recorded and reviewable

We wrote two detectors for headings: one for lines starting with known title words, one that checks whether every word in a short line is a Raag name or a musical term — the Raag list being built from the project's own metadata file.

Both had bugs, and both were caught by testing rather than by reading the code:

  • Punjabi vowel marks broke the word-boundary rules. In Gurmukhi, vowel signs are separate marks attached to letters, and the programming tool we used does not count them as part of a word. Several heading types were slipping through as a result.
  • The rules were deleting real scripture. The Mool Mantar — the opening of the entire Granth — was being removed as a heading, along with 14 genuine verses that happened to begin with words like ਘਰੁ or ਰਾਗੁ.

The fix for the second was a rule we called the scripture guard: if a line is substantial and all three scholars wrote an explanation of it, it is scripture — whatever word it begins with. Scholars do not write commentary on chapter headings.

The check that confirmed it

Of the 202 headings the vocabulary rule newly caught, 194 had only one commentary attached — and that one was the mechanical kind ("Sarang, Fourth Master, Dupada"). Genuine verses have three real explanations. The evidence agreed with the rule.

Nothing was deleted. 5,077 lines were flagged and written to a separate review file; 55,217 remained as usable scripture.

3

Building the teaching material

164,778 examples, and one trap avoided

Here is the central idea of the whole project, and it is worth understanding properly.

We have no data that says "verse A and verse B mean the same thing." Nobody has ever written that list. But we do have, for every verse, a scholar's explanation of what it means in plain modern Punjabi.

So we pair each verse with its explanations. The system learns to associate a verse with its meaning. And then something useful follows on its own: if verse A and verse B mean the same thing, their explanations will look alike, so both verses get pulled toward the same place — and toward each other.

The trap: an exam you have already seen

Any learning system must be tested on material it has not been taught. The obvious method — shuffle everything, teach with 80%, test with 20% — fails badly here.

Lines of a single Shabad are near-repetitions of one another. Shuffle randomly and line 3 goes into the teaching set while line 4 goes into the exam. The system then appears to understand line 4 when in fact it memorised line 3.

So we grouped instead. Verses stay together if they belong to the same Shabad, if they are the same verse repeated elsewhere (the Mool Mantar appears 29 times), or if they share an identical commentary. Whole groups go to teaching or to testing, never split.

We checked rather than assumed — and the first attempt leaked

Twenty-nine commentaries had slipped across the boundary. Small, but it created impossible exam questions: the system taught that a commentary meant verse A, then marked wrong for not answering verse B. Adding a third grouping rule closed it. All twelve overlap checks now return zero.

Final result: 131,806 teaching examples, 16,457 for checking during training, 16,515 held back for the final exam — and evenly balanced across all three scholars, so no single voice dominates.

4

Turning verses into numbers

The one stage that uses a neural model

A computer cannot compare meanings directly. What it can do is turn each verse into a long list of numbers — 1,024 of them — positioned so that verses with similar meanings end up with similar lists. These lists are called embeddings.

In plain terms

Picture every verse as a pin on an enormous map. The map is arranged so that verses about envy land in one region and verses about fear land in another. Finding related verses is then just looking at which pins are nearby. The 1,024 numbers are a verse's coordinates on that map.

The model that produces those coordinates is BGE-M3, published by the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence. It was chosen because it handles over 100 languages including Punjabi, was built specifically for search rather than chat, and is freely available.

Placing all 54,844 verses on the map took 112 minutes on the project laptop. It is done once and saved.

5

Building an exam nobody had written

919 questions, mined from scholarship

To prove the system improves, we must be able to mark its answers. But no list exists of which verses mean the same thing. So we built one from the commentaries.

The rule: two verses are a matching pair only when two independent scholars — Sahib Singh and Manmohan Singh, different centuries, different styles — both explain them in similar words. One scholar alone is not enough.

Why two scholars, not one

Because one scholar's habits produce false matches. Both write ਕਬੀਰ ਆਖਦਾ ਹੈ ("Kabir says") whenever a verse opens that way — so two unrelated verses looked identical, purely because of a shared turn of phrase. We stripped those speech formulas out; the false pair's score collapsed from 0.53 to 0.00, and genuine pairs were untouched.

We also required the answers to come from a different Shabad, because showing someone the next line of the passage they are already reading is not a discovery.

Two honest limitations we record openly: the exam is derived from commentaries, and it can only find pairs whose commentaries share vocabulary — so it certainly misses valid answers. Every score below is therefore a floor, not a ceiling. Because every method is marked against the identical exam, comparisons between them remain fair.

Did we train anything? No — and that was deliberate

This is the question that matters most, so it is worth stating without hedging: no model has been trained on Gurbani yet. Everything measured so far uses BGE-M3 exactly as its authors released it.

That is intentional. To claim that teaching the model Gurbani improved it, you must know how it performed before. Measure only after training and the number stands alone — perhaps the model was always that good. The comparison is the result, so the "before" must be recorded first.

The finding: a leading AI model loses to keyword search

BGE-M3 scored 19.37%. Plain letter-matching scored 27.09%. One of the strongest multilingual search models available is worse than counting letters on this task.

On the 420 hardest questions, every method is under 1.5%. Nothing currently works.

Examining its successes explains why. Of 919 questions, the untrained model makes a genuine leap of meaning — correct answer near the top, sharing no words with the question — exactly four times. Its other successes are near-identical verses with spelling differences, like ਅਸਤਿ ਏਕ ਦਿਗਰਿ ਕੁਈ matched to ਅਸਤਿ ਏਕੁ ਦਿਗਰ ਕੁਈ.

It is recognising shapes of words, not meanings. The clearest demonstration came from a live search:

SEARCHED FOR
ਢਾਢੀ ਗੁਣ ਗਾਵੈ ਨਿਤ ਜਨਮੁ ਸਵਾਰਿਆ ॥
"The bard sings His praises always — his life is redeemed."
RETURNED AS A CLOSE MATCH   Opposite meaning
ਧ੍ਰਿਗੁ ਜੀਵਣੁ ਬਿਰਥਾ ਜਨਮੁ ਗਵਾਇਆ ॥
"His life is accursed — he has wasted it in vain."
What went wrong

One verse says a life was redeemed; the other says a life was wasted. They are opposites. The model ranked them together because both contain ਜਨਮੁ ("life") and because ਸਵਾਰਿਆ and ਗਵਾਇਆ look alike. It has never been taught what these words mean in Gurbani.

This failure is not a setback — it is the justification. The project's premise, that existing tools cannot retrieve Gurbani by meaning, is now supported by measurement rather than assertion.

What happens next

The remaining work is teaching BGE-M3 the Gurbani material we prepared. This requires a graphics card: the model has 568 million internal settings, and adjusting them on the project laptop would take weeks. An RTX 3060 is available and the training program is written and checked.

  • Train on the 131,806 examples — one to three hours on the graphics card.
  • Re-place all verses on the map using the newly taught model — minutes.
  • Re-sit the identical exam. The results table gains a fourth row; the difference between it and the third row is the project's result.
  • Then improve further — show the model its own mistakes and retrain, add a second-pass ranker, and finally have a language model write the explanation a reader sees.

Two numbers must be beaten: 27.09% overall, and 1.43% on the hardest questions. Both were measured, on this exam, on this laptop — and both are written down.
